# One job, two agents

A coordinator takes a job, splits it, hires two specialists for the parts, and
each one is paid for its own piece. Every rule is enforced by Kaspa rather than
by anybody's good behaviour, and every step leaves a transaction a stranger can
check.

`growth/tools/one-job.ts` runs it end to end on testnet-10.

---

## The job

Two pieces, two sellers, two different kinds of work:

| Worker | Buys | From | Price cap |
|---|---|---|---|
| RESEARCH | a checkable record about a repository | Growth Researcher, `/verify` | 0.05 KAS |
| VERIFY | an audit of Warda's own grant covenant | Covenant Auditor, `POST /v1/report` | 0.04 KAS |

Both sellers were already running before this existed and neither was told
anything about it. VERIFY posts `covenant/warda_grant.sil` and gets a report
back, so the thing being audited is the thing that enforced the payment.

## The keys

Four, and the fourth is why the rest can be trusted.

```
coord.key      signs genesis and both delegations. Never signs a purchase.
research.key   signs RESEARCH's purchase. Nothing else.
verify.key     signs VERIFY's purchase. Nothing else.
ops/warda-revocation.key
               stops any of it, at any moment, and receives nothing.
```

The coordinator key is generated by the run and lives in `one-job/keys/`, which
`*.key` in `.gitignore` keeps out of the repo. The revocation key is the one
`ops/known-keys.json` names for grants issued from here on — the same key
`ops/grants.ts` uses, so this run does not quietly name a different stopper.

If the coordinator and a worker shared a key, that worker would hold the parent
grant too, and "RESEARCH cannot pay the Auditor" would be a claim about which
file the script read rather than about what the chain allows.

## The terms

```
coordinator   budget 0.80 KAS   maxPerSpend 0.05   depth 2   term ~3 days
              payees: the Researcher and the Auditor, and nobody else, ever
RESEARCH      budget 0.25 KAS   maxPerSpend 0.05   epoch 0.15   term ~1 day
              payee: the Researcher ONLY
VERIFY        budget 0.22 KAS   maxPerSpend 0.04   epoch 0.12   term ~1 day
              payee: the Auditor ONLY
```

The caps are the sellers' listed prices to the sompi. The **budgets** are not
about the job at all — they are the smallest amounts the network will let a
worker pay out of, with headroom. See the pre-flight below.

Each child is narrowed to exactly one payee out of a parent that may pay two.
That is the subset witness: the child commits to a *node* of the parent's
allowlist tree and carries the path from that node to the parent's root, so the
narrowing is proved rather than promised. RESEARCH cannot pay the Auditor,
VERIFY cannot pay the Researcher, and neither can pay anyone else at any price,
however their code is written.

A worker's term outlives the job by hours, not weeks, so a key that leaks after
the job is a key with a day to live and one address to spend at.

## Running it

```
source ops/node.env
node --experimental-strip-types growth/tools/one-job.ts --submit
```

Without `--submit` it prints the mass table and exits 2. `--dir <name>` puts a
run in its own directory, so a fresh name is a fresh run.

It needs a funded funder key whose **largest single coin** covers 0.5 KAS —
genesis takes one input, so a wallet with plenty spread over small coins is a
wallet that cannot open this grant. `warda wallet` shows the spread.

It also needs the Auditor answering. If it is down, the run stops at
`buy-verify` with everything before it intact.

### Steps

`keys → genesis → open → hire-research → hire-verify → buy-research →
buy-verify → settle-verify → settle-research → close → trace`

Every step records itself in `one-job/state.json`. A step that already ran is
skipped, so re-running after a failure costs the rest of that step and nothing
before it — which is what matters when the failure lands after a genesis that
already broadcast.

Exit code 4 means **paid and not served**: the money moved and the goods did
not arrive. Do not re-run. Resolve it with `agents/tools/buy.ts` against the
recorded purchase in `one-job/purchases/`.

### The storage-mass pre-flight, and why the budgets are what they are

Before it writes a key, the script masses every transaction the plan implies
and refuses if any of them is over the ceiling:

```
storage mass, against a ceiling of 500000:
  delegate research    185329
  delegate verify      243476
  buy research         262222
  buy verify           318182
  settle verify             0
  settle research           0
```

This check exists because its absence cost a run. On 24 September 2026 the
first attempt got through a genesis and two delegations and was refused at the
first purchase: VERIFY held 0.12 KAS, its 0.04 KAS payment massed 583,334
against a ceiling of 500,000, and no money moved. The covenant was fine — this
is consensus, and no grant term relaxes it.

**The rule is not a floor under the payment.** 0.04 KAS is far above any dust
limit. Mass counts `1/value` over a transaction's *outputs*, and a grant that
pays out creates two: the payment, and the grant's own successor. A covenant
output carries a 32-byte binding that pushes it into a second storage unit and
squares its weight, so the successor dominates — which means **what costs is
what the grant has left**. A nearly-empty grant is the expensive one.

Concretely, at these fees: paying 0.04 KAS needs about 0.133 KAS in the child
grant, and paying 0.05 KAS needs about 0.138. The larger payment is the cheaper
transaction, because it comes out of a larger grant. Anything that reads this
as a dust limit gets it backwards, which is exactly how the first run was
sized.

The arithmetic lives in `growth/src/one-job-plan.ts` and is pinned by
`growth/test/one-job-plan.test.ts` against the 583,334 the network quoted. A
mass calculation that stops reproducing that number has stopped describing the
network.

### Why settlement runs backwards

`batch.ts settle` pops the reserve chain from the end, and a child settled out
of order is refused before anything is built. VERIFY was hired second, so it
comes home first. This is not a preference in the script; it is the shape of
the chain of commitments.

## What it produces

`one-job/trace.json` — the coordinator manifest, the plan, both sellers, every
step with its txid, and the batch log. Every figure on any page built from this
traces to a transaction this run broadcast.

## Three things this does NOT show, all deliberate

**It is not atomic.** Everyone joining one agreement in a single step is the
shape people usually describe when they talk about agents hiring agents, and it
is exactly what the covenant forbids: `#[covenant.fanout(to = 2)]` with
`require(OpAuthOutputCount == 2)` fixes the topology at parent plus ONE child.
Two workers is two delegations, in two transactions.

**They are not even concurrent.** A grant is one UTXO, so the second
delegation's input is the first one's output and does not exist until it
confirms. Hiring is serial by construction, not by scheduling.

**The workers had to be known before the job existed.** A grant's payee set is
fixed at genesis and a child may only narrow it, so a coordinator can hire from
the cast it was created with and nobody else. An agent discovered at run time
cannot be paid, at any delegation depth. That is the honest distance between
this and an open market, and no amount of tooling closes it — it needs the
covenant to change.

Also true and less interesting: a parent's rate limit does not bind its
subtree. `delegate` requires only `child.epochLimit <= epochLimit`, per child,
so a parent capped at five per epoch can hire ten children capped at five each
and the subtree spends fifty. The coordinator's budget is what bounds the job;
its epoch limit bounds only its own direct spending, and it does none.

What a settlement does give back is the reserve: `reabsorb` requires
`newState.reserved == reserved - child.budgetTotal`, so the two settle steps
restore the coordinator's capacity to delegate again. That is why this run can
close cleanly rather than ending with half a budget stranded.
