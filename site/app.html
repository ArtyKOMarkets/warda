02 The rule

Add this to ops/alerts.json on the machine beside your node. That file is gitignored: it names your addresses and the exact figure you are watching for, and a published threshold is a number somebody knows to sit just underneath.

—

Only changes are sent, including the change back. A message every fifteen minutes for a condition that is still true is a message you learn to swipe away, and the one that mattered goes with it. A rule that cannot be read — the node is down, or the chain holds nothing where the manifest says the grant is — says so once, and is never reported as “fine”.

03 Where it runs

On your machine, next to your node. Nothing here is stored on a server, and this page has no way to reach your chat.

cp ops/alerts.env.example ops/alerts.env   # your bot token and chat id
ops/alerts.sh --test                       # prove the pipe works, before you rely on it
ops/alerts.sh --dry-run                    # what it would send, sending nothing
ops/install-cron.sh                        # every fifteen minutes from now on

It notifies. It never acts. The obvious next feature is the one it will not have: converting for you when the balance reaches your number. That needs a key that can move the coin, held unattended, forever — which is the hot wallet this whole project exists to not be. So the strongest thing to say about the alerter is what it cannot do: it holds no key, signs nothing, and builds no transaction, and the build fails if that stops being true.

02 Its keys, which this page never sees

Three powers, three keys, because they are three separate risks. The agent's is generated by the command in step 4, on your machine, and written to agent.key — it is never typed, printed or sent anywhere. The revocation key you make yourself and paste only the public half of. Nothing secret is entered here; this console cannot sign and has no way to.

warda key --out revocation.key   # can stop it at any moment, and receives nothing

Left blank, the stop is the funding key itself. That is one key to keep and one thing to lose — fine for a first grant, wrong for anything left running.

The revocation key is the one worth keeping cold. A revoke pays the principal rather than its own signer, so whoever holds it can end a grant and cannot take a sompi of it. That asymmetry is the entire reason the two are separate.

03 Funding it

You sell what you already hold, wherever it already has a market, and withdraw the proceeds. Warda does not touch the asset, does not quote the price and never holds the float — so there is no balance sheet between you and your agent.

—

Genesis takes a single input. The figure that matters is your largest coin, not your balance — an exchange that splits a withdrawal into two payments funds nothing, and nobody has any reason to expect that. This watches for the coin, not the total, and says which of the two you have.

04 Creating it

One command, on your machine, with your key. It builds the grant locally, writes the manifest before it broadcasts, and prints the address the coin will live at.

—

Keep the manifest and the allowlist. A grant's address is derived from the numbers in that file and nowhere else, and the allowlist is what a spend rebuilds its proof from. Lose either and the grant can be revoked, never spent.
